# Supporting Clerical and Administrative Workers Through Australia's AI Transition

**Prepared for:** Australian Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)  
**Policy focus:** Targeted support for clerical and administrative workers affected by generative AI

## Executive Summary

## Problem Definition and Policy Context

Prepared for the Australian Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR), this proposal addresses unequal adjustment costs from generative AI for clerical and administrative workers, particularly women and mature-aged employees. Their jobs contain routine information-processing tasks that generative AI can automate, but exposure is not equivalent to job loss: Jobs and Skills Australia finds that augmentation generally outweighs automation, while automation potential is concentrated in routine roles (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). The early labour-market signal still warrants action. DEWR finds no broad AI-driven upheaval, yet occupations with greater automation exposure have experienced slower employment growth; it stresses that this association is suggestive, not causal (DEWR 2026). Acting early can reduce displacement and widening digital and gender gaps, while targeting is needed to avoid subsidising training unrelated to real job transitions. The proposal therefore operationalises Action 5 of the National AI Plan, which commits government to skills development, career support and workforce mobility, with particular attention to women and mature-aged workers (DISR 2025).

## Labour Market Impacts

Generative AI changes labour demand at the task level before eliminating whole occupations. In clerical work, summarising documents, entering data, classifying records and producing standard customer responses can be automated; exception handling, interpersonal judgement and accountability remain complementary human tasks. When lower prices or better service expand demand, employment may rise; with fixed output, firms can instead use fewer workers, causing slower recruitment or attrition-led displacement before retrenchments appear (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025).

The adjustment burden is uneven. Accounting Clerks have high automation exposure, an 81 per cent female workforce and a median age of 45; for General Clerks, the figures are 84 per cent and 42 (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). Displacement could therefore reinforce gender inequality and impose larger retraining costs on mid-career workers with firm-specific experience. ABS data show a 6.8 per cent job-leaving rate for clerical and administrative workers in the year to February 2026; among those changing jobs, 38 per cent moved to a different major occupation group (ABS 2026). Mobility exists, but transition costs remain.

The productivity upside is substantial but its distribution is not automatic. The Productivity Commission estimates that AI could increase Australian labour productivity by about 4.3 per cent over a decade, while emphasising considerable uncertainty (Productivity Commission 2025). OECD evidence shows that an AI assistant raised novice customer-support productivity by 34 per cent, suggesting that well-designed augmentation can narrow skill gaps (Filippucci et al. 2024). However, benefits may instead flow to profits or AI-skilled employees, while remaining staff face work intensification, monitoring and weaker bargaining power. Policy should therefore complement adoption with portable skills, worker participation in job redesign and support tied to sustained employment and wage outcomes.

## Policy Options and Evaluation

Three options are assessed against efficiency, equity, fiscal cost and administrative feasibility. Costings are illustrative two-year Commonwealth estimates for refinement during program design.

| Option | Design and indicative cost | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **1. AI Transition Training Accounts** | Offer 50,000 workers in high-exposure clerical occupations up to $2,000 for approved TAFE AI and digital training, including paid study time. Adding 10 per cent for administration and evaluation gives **$110 million**. | Portable support reaches workers before displacement and corrects under-investment in transferable skills. Existing TAFE channels aid feasibility (DISR 2025). Broad eligibility, however, creates deadweight and course-to-job mismatch risks. |
| **2. Targeted transition and placement service** | Extend Career Transition Assistance and Workforce Australia for 20,000 at-risk or displaced workers, combining skills assessment, recognition of prior learning, coaching and placement. At $4,000 each plus 10 per cent administration, the cost is **$88 million**. | Targeting reduces deadweight and advances equity for women and workers aged 45 and over, while using an established mature-age service (DEWR 2022). Per-person costs and provider-quality risks are higher, and support may arrive too late if displacement determines eligibility. |
| **3. Employer AI Adoption Agreements** | Offer matched grants averaging $35,000 to 2,000 employers for paid training, worker consultation and job redesign, conditional on redeployment and reporting. Adding $10 million for administration and evaluation gives **$80 million**. | Work-linked training improves task fit and preserves firm-specific knowledge. Yet grants can subsidise adoption that would occur anyway, favour firms with application capacity, and let employers capture benefits unless consultation and job-quality conditions are enforced. |

Option 1 is most scalable; Option 2 is most equitable and measurable; Option 3 best connects skills with productivity but has the greatest capture risk. Because no option covers both prevention and job matching, a staged package should combine their strongest features.

## Recommended Package and Implementation

DEWR should establish a two-year **AI Transition Compact** as a capped $150 million pilot. Rather than funding all three options at full scale, the Compact would combine 30,000 training accounts at up to $2,000 each ($60 million), 15,000 transition-service places at $4,000 each ($60 million), and 500 matched employer agreements averaging $35,000 ($17.5 million). The remaining $12.5 million would fund administration, data linkage and independent evaluation. Eligibility would require current employment, or employment within the previous 12 months, in a clerical occupation on an annually updated high-exposure list (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). Women, workers aged 45 and over, regional workers and people assessed as having low digital confidence would receive priority, but not exclusive, access.

DEWR would lead delivery with Jobs and Skills Australia, state training authorities, TAFEs, Workforce Australia providers, unions and employer bodies. This uses existing training and mature-age transition infrastructure while adding an AI-specific pathway (DEWR 2022; DISR 2025). During months 1–6, DEWR would define approved courses, contract providers and co-design grant conditions. A staged rollout would operate during months 7–24, with an interim review after month 12. Training payments would be released partly on completion. Employer grants would require 1:1 co-contributions, paid training time, worker consultation, a redeployment-before-retrenchment plan and de-identified outcome reporting.

The primary outcome should be sustained employment 26 weeks after support, compared with similar eligible non-participants through a pre-specified matched comparison and staggered rollout. Secondary measures should include course completion, digital-skill gains, earnings retention, occupational mobility, displacement and employer-reported task productivity. All outcomes should be reported by gender, age and region, alongside cost per additional sustained job. Continuation beyond two years should depend on independently verified improvements in employment or earnings that are large enough to justify fiscal cost, with weak courses and providers removed rather than automatically renewed.

## Risks and Mitigations

Key risks are deadweight funding, low-quality credentials, inequitable take-up, provider gaming and employer capture of subsidies. DEWR should restrict approved courses using labour-market evidence, require employer co-contributions, link part of provider payments to completion and outcomes, audit performance, and reserve outreach and support for priority groups. A staged pilot and pre-registered evaluation would limit fiscal exposure. Privacy risks from linked records should be managed through data minimisation, de-identification, restricted access, retention limits and published governance.
